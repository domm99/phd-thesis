\section{Collective Adaptive Systems}

A \glsentryfull{CAS} is a system whose behavior results
 from the interactions among multiple autonomous or semi-autonomous entities 
 and that changes during operation in response to variations in either the system itself 
 or its environment~\cite{DBLP:conf/huc/Ferscha15}. 
% 
The term identifies two complementary properties. 
%
A system is \emph{collective} when its function cannot be attributed to any component in isolation, 
 but depends on how multiple components interact and coordinate. 
% 
It is \emph{adaptive} when it can modify the mechanisms that govern its behavior, 
 rather than merely produce different outputs according to a fixed set of rules. 
% 
Adaptation may affect parameters, decision policies, interaction patterns, role assignments, 
 or the organization of the collective itself.

This definition describes a broad class of systems. 
%
Their components may be software agents, sensors, mobile devices, robots, vehicles, users, or services;
 they may be homogeneous or differ in capabilities and responsibilities; 
 and their interactions may take place in a physical environment, a communication network, or both. 
%
What makes them a collective is not their mere coexistence,
 but the presence of a system-level function or behavior sustained by their interdependence. 
% 
A collection of unrelated devices is therefore not a CAS,
 whereas a population whose members exchange information, divide work, 
 or jointly affect a shared environment can be considered one even when 
 no member has a complete representation of the system.

\glspl{CAS} can be viewed as a manifestation of the transition from computing 
 with individual machines to computing through ensembles of networked entities. 
 
In the collective-computing perspective, 
 computation is no longer confined to a single device or delivered independently by many devices; 
 it is produced by their many-to-many interactions~\cite{DBLP:journals/computer/Abowd}. 
% 
This shift changes the primary object of engineering. 
%
The behavior of each component remains relevant, but it must be understood in relation
 to the organization and dynamics of the ensemble to which it belongs.

\subsection{The Micro--Macro Relationship}
\label{subsec:cas-micro-macro}

The distinction between the \emph{micro} and \emph{macro} levels 
 is central to the characterization of a CAS. 
% 
The micro level comprises the state, observations, decisions, 
 and direct interactions of the individual entities.
%
The macro level concerns properties attributed to the collective as a whole,
 such as spatial coverage, consensus, a partition into functional regions, 
 the motion of a swarm, or the quality of a model learned from distributed data.
%
These properties are not stored or controlled by a single component in the general case. 
%
Instead, 
 they are induced by many local computations 
 and by the information that propagates through the interaction network.

The relationship between the two levels is bidirectional. 
%
Local decisions produce and continuously reshape macroscopic structures, 
 while those structures alter the context in which subsequent local decisions are made.
%
For instance,
 the selection of a coordinator by nearby devices creates a region at the macro level; 
 membership in that region then determines the information available to each device 
 and the agents with which it cooperates. 
% 
Similarly, 
 local placement or routing decisions can create congestion, 
 which in turn changes the cost of future decisions. 
% 
A \gls{CAS} is therefore better understood as a coupled feedback process 
 than as a static collection of components.

System boundaries are also dependent on the level 
 and purpose of the analysis.
%
A collective may contain transient sub-collectives, 
 and the same entity may participate in several groups associated with different functions. 
% 
Conversely,
 an entire collective may act as one component of a larger system. 
% 
This nesting is particularly common in edge--cloud environments, 
 where devices may form local coordination groups while simultaneously interacting with shared edge 
 and cloud infrastructure. 
% 
Consequently, membership, neighborhood, and role should generally be treated 
 as dynamic properties rather than fixed design-time facts.

\subsection{Adaptation, Self-Organization, and Emergence}
\label{subsec:cas-adaptation}

Adaptation in a \gls{CAS} may occur at both the individual and collective levels. 
%
An individual entity may update a model, revise a decision policy, 
 change its operating parameters, or migrate a computation. 
% 
At the collective level, 
 the system may modify its communication topology, redistribute responsibilities,
 form or dissolve groups, elect coordinators, or relocate services. 
% 
These forms of adaptation are often interdependent: 
 a change in an individual model can alter which entities should cooperate, 
 while a change in collective organization determines the observations 
 and resources available to each learning process.

The ability to adapt is commonly expressed through \emph{self-*} properties.
%
Self-configuration concerns the autonomous selection of a suitable configuration; 
 self-optimization aims to improve performance during operation;
 self-healing enables recovery from failures or degraded conditions; and
 self-protection concerns the detection and mitigation of threats. 
% 
At the level of a collective, 
 these properties cannot generally be reduced to independent actions performed by its members. 
% 
Recovery, for example, 
 may require the redistribution of a failed component's responsibilities, 
 while optimization may require balancing competing local improvements 
 against their system-wide effects.

\emph{Self-organization} 
 denotes the process through which a system creates 
 and maintains an ordered structure without relying on an external controller. 
% 
In a \gls{CAS}, 
 this structure may consist of neighborhoods, hierarchies, spatial regions, coalitions, 
 or other patterns of interaction. 
% 
Self-organization is especially valuable when the population 
 and its environment change too frequently for a fixed organization to remain appropriate. 
% 
It is nevertheless a means rather than an objective: 
 the resulting structure is useful only insofar as it supports the intended collective behavior.

Self-organization is closely related to, but distinct from, \emph{emergence}.
%
Emergence describes the appearance of coherent macro-level properties 
 from interactions at the micro level, 
 where those properties cannot be directly assigned to any individual component. 
% 
The emergent outcome need not be beneficial or intended. 
%
The same feedback mechanisms that yield coordination may also produce 
oscillations, congestion, fragmentation, or other undesirable effects. 
%
Engineering a \gls{CAS} therefore requires both enabling desired emergent
 behavior and constraining the conditions under which harmful behavior can develop.

Adaptation also raises the question of stability. 
%
A system that reacts to every local variation 
 may continually reorganize  without converging to a useful state,
 while one that adapts too slowly may remain ineffective after 
 its operating conditions have changed. 
%
One relevant objective is \emph{self-stabilization}:
 after a transient perturbation, the collective should recover a state that satisfies 
 its intended properties without requiring global reinitialization.
%
Self-stabilizing coordination mechanisms provide a foundation for resilient \glspl{CAS}
 because they tolerate changes in local state, failures, and network topology
 while preserving system-level behavior~\cite{DBLP:journals/tomacs/ViroliABDP18}.

\subsection{Characteristic Properties and Engineering Implications}
\label{subsec:cas-properties}

Although \glspl{CAS} encompass different application domains, 
 several recurring properties shape how they can be engineered.

\paragraph{Situatedness and locality.}
The entities of a \gls{CAS} operate within a logical or physical environment 
 from which they obtain observations and on which their actions have effects. 
%
Access to this environment is normally local: 
 an entity observes only part of the relevant state and communicates with a limited neighborhood. 
% 
Locality improves scalability and permits timely decisions, 
 but prevents any component from assuming a continuously available global view. 
% 
Information about collective conditions must instead be estimated, propagated, 
 or aggregated through the system.

\paragraph{Scale and openness.}
\glspl{CAS} may contain hundreds or thousands of entities, 
 and their population is often open. 
% 
Components can join, leave, move, fail, or become temporarily unreachable, 
 changing both the system size and its interaction topology.
%
Algorithms that depend on a fixed membership, 
 a stable ordering of entities, or global synchronization become increasingly fragile under these conditions.
%
Collective mechanisms should instead operate incrementally 
 and tolerate asynchrony and partial participation.

\paragraph{Autonomy and decentralized control.}
Individual entities retain control over at least part of their state 
 and behavior. 
% 
Their decisions are based on local knowledge 
 and may be made without authorization from a permanent central coordinator. 
% 
Decentralization removes single points of control, distributes computational load, 
 and can improve responsiveness and fault tolerance. 
% 
At the same time, 
 it makes global correctness harder to establish: 
 locally reasonable decisions can conflict, and delayed or inconsistent information 
 can prevent coordination or slow convergence.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity.}
The members of a CAS may differ in hardware, energy availability,
 communication capacity, data, mobility, and assigned role. 
% 
Heterogeneity can expand the capabilities of the collective, 
 since different components contribute complementary resources and observations. 
% 
It also prevents uniform assumptions about what each component can compute, communicate, or learn. 
%
Coordination mechanisms must therefore distinguish differences 
 that are operationally relevant while avoiding dependencies on the identity 
 or ordering of individual entities.

\paragraph{Resilience.}
Failures and environmental changes are part of the expected operating conditions of a \gls{CAS} 
 rather than exceptional events. 
% 
Resilience consequently does not mean preventing every disruption, 
 but maintaining acceptable system-level behavior and recovering when the collective is perturbed.
%
Redundant components and decentralized control can support graceful degradation, 
 although redundancy alone is insufficient: 
 the collective must also detect changes and
 reorganize its activity around the resources that remain available.

These properties create an important engineering trade-off. 
%
Local interaction, decentralization, and openness enable scale and robustness, 
 but also make the system difficult to predict, verify, and control. 
%
Evaluation must consequently consider both levels of the system. 
%
Component-level measures such as latency, energy consumption, 
 or individual accuracy remain necessary, 
 but they do not by themselves establish that the collective satisfies its intended function.
%
Macro-level properties such as coverage, convergence, fairness, stability, 
 and resource utilization must be assessed as outcomes of the interactions among components.

\subsection{Relationship with Adjacent Paradigms}
\label{subsec:cas-related-paradigms}

\glspl{CAS} overlap with several established classes of systems,
 but are not interchangeable with them. 
%
\gls{MAS} provide models and mechanisms
 for autonomy, interaction, coordination, and organization. 
% 
A \gls{CAS} can therefore ften be modeled as a\gls{MAS}. 
%
The \gls{CAS} perspective, however, 
 places particular emphasis on how macroscopic behavior 
 and adaptation arise in potentially large, open, and dynamically structured populations.

Swarm systems represent a more specialized case. 
%
They typically comprise many relatively simple 
 and homogeneous agents that follow decentralized rules,
 producing robust and scalable behavior through redundancy 
 and local interaction~\cite{DBLP:journals/swarm/BrambillaFBD13}. 
% 
\glspl{CAS} retain these principles but also include heterogeneous entities, 
 explicit organizational roles, virtual services, and combinations of centralized 
 and decentralized mechanisms. 
% 
Accordingly, 
 an adaptive robotic swarm can be regarded as a specialized \gls{CAS}, 
 but a \gls{CAS} need not exhibit the uniformity 
 or bio-inspired organization commonly associated with swarms.

The distinction from self-adaptive systems concerns the locus of adaptation. 
%
A self-adaptive system may be a single software system 
 whose behavior is changed by a centralized adaptation manager. 
% 
In a \gls{CAS}, 
 adaptation is distributed across multiple interacting entities 
 and may also modify the relationships among them.
%
Likewise, 
 the Internet of Things and cyber-physical systems describe how computational entities are connected 
 to one another and to the physical world, but connectivity and physical embodiment do not alone imply 
 collective adaptation. 
%
They provide common substrates on which \glspl{CAS} can be realized.

\subsection{Engineering Perspective Adopted in This Thesis}
\label{subsec:cas-thesis-perspective}

The systems considered in this thesis are cooperative many-agent \glspl{CAS}. 
%
Their members operate with partial observations, 
 communicate primarily with a time-varying local neighborhood, 
 and contribute to system-level objectives while retaining local state, data, and resource constraints. 
% 
Cooperation does not require every entity to perform the same action or maintain the same model.
%
Rather, 
 it requires mechanisms through which locally available information 
 and capabilities can be organized into behavior that benefits the collective.

Within this scope, 
 learning is one mechanism of adaptation, 
 while organization and deployment constitute two others. 
% 
Models and policies adapt from data and experience; 
 cooperation structures adapt to determine which entities exchange knowledge; 
 and deployment decisions adapt the placement of computation to the available edge--cloud resources. 
% 
These processes cannot be designed in isolation, 
 because each changes the context observed by the others. 
% 
The central engineering problem is therefore to express and control their interaction 
 at the level of the collective while preserving decentralized execution.

This perspective motivates the two complementary foundations developed in the following chapters. 
%
Macroprogramming provides abstractions for specifying collective behavior 
 without prescribing every local interaction individually~\cite{DBLP:journals/csur/Casadei23}, 
 whereas machine learning provides mechanisms for deriving models 
 and decisions when the relevant conditions cannot be exhaustively specified at design time. 
% 
Their integration supports \glspl{CAS} in which learning is not an isolated function executed 
 by independent devices, but a coordinated and adaptive part of the collective behavior.


\section{Edge-Cloud Continuum}

The \glsentryfull{ECC} is a distributed computing environment 
 in which computation, data, and services can be placed across resources 
 ranging from end devices, through intermediate edge infrastructure, to remote cloud 
 data centers~\cite{DBLP:journals/jpdc/RosendoCVA22}. 
% 
The term \emph{continuum} emphasizes that these resources form a common deployment space 
 rather than a collection of isolated platforms. 
% 
An application function may execute where its data are produced, on a nearby server, in the cloud, 
 or across several of these locations. 
% 
Its placement may also change during operation as resource availability, 
 connectivity, workload, and application requirements evolve.

The continuum therefore concerns more than the coexistence of edge and cloud resources. 
%
It introduces the possibility of selecting where each part of an application should execute 
 and of revising that selection over time. 
% 
Different locations provide different combinations of computational capacity, response time, 
 energy consumption, data locality, availability, and monetary cost. 
% 
The central engineering problem is to exploit this diversity without exposing 
 the application to every infrastructural detail or assuming 
 that one location is always preferable.

This perspective emerged in response to the limitations 
 of exclusively cloud-centered architectures. 
% 
Large cloud data centers offer elastic computation, storage, and global availability, 
 but reaching them requires communication across the wide-area network.
% 
For applications coupled to physical processes, the resulting latency, bandwidth consumption, 
intermittent connectivity, and transfer of potentially sensitive data may be unacceptable. 
%
Edge computing complements the cloud by moving selected computations closer to their data sources 
 and actuators~\cite{DBLP:journals/computer/Satyanarayanan17}. 
% 
It does not eliminate the cloud; instead, 
 the continuum combines the responsiveness and contextual proximity of the edge with the capacity 
 and global reach of centralized infrastructure.

\subsection{From a Tiered Architecture to a Continuum}
\label{subsec:ecc-architecture}

The \gls{ECC} is commonly introduced through three regions: 
 end devices, edge servers, and cloud servers (\Cref{fig:???}). 
% 
This organization is useful for reasoning about their typical roles, 
 but the boundaries between the regions are not absolute. 
% 
Devices at the same nominal tier may differ by several orders of magnitude in capacity, 
 and intermediate resources may combine properties of both the edge and the cloud. 
% 
The continuum is consequently better understood in terms of relative proximity, capabilities, 
 and responsibilities than as a rigid three-level hierarchy~\cite{DBLP:journals/jpdc/RosendoCVA22}.

\paragraph{End devices.}
End devices are the resources closest to the physical or digital phenomena of interest. 
%
They include sensors, smartphones, embedded computers, vehicles, robots, 
 and other devices that generate data or produce effects in an environment. 
% 
Their proximity provides immediate access to local context 
 and can support low-latency operation even when external connectivity is unavailable.
%
However, 
 these devices often have limited processors, memory, energy, and thermal capacity. 
% 
Application computation must also share those resources 
 with sensing, communication, and control responsibilities.

\paragraph{Edge infrastructure.}
The edge comprises resources located between end devices 
 and remote data centers, such as gateways, access points, base stations, on-premises servers, 
 and small regional clusters. 
% 
Edge nodes aggregate demand from nearby devices 
 and offer greater capacity without incurring the full latency of cloud access. 
% 
Their location also allows them to retain data within a geographic or administrative domain. 
%
Nevertheless, 
 edge capacity is finite and shared: an edge server that is well suited to one device 
 may become overloaded when many nearby devices make the same placement decision.

\paragraph{Cloud infrastructure.}
Cloud data centers provide comparatively abundant computation and storage,
 elastic provisioning, and a global view that is valuable for expensive analysis,
 long-term persistence, and the coordination of geographically dispersed applications. 
% 
These benefits come at the cost of physical distance and dependence on the intervening network. 
%
Cloud execution is therefore attractive for resource-intensive or delay-tolerant functions, 
 but less suitable when decisions must be made with tight latency bounds or during disruptions in connectivity.

Several related terms emphasize particular portions of this environment.
%
\emph{Mobile} or \emph{multi-access edge computing} places resources 
 within the access network, especially to support mobile clients, 
 whereas \emph{fog computing} typically stresses a geographically distributed hierarchy of intermediate 
 nodes~\cite{DBLP:journals/comsur/MachB17,DBLP:journals/jpdc/RosendoCVA22}.
%
In this thesis, 
 \emph{edge} is used in the broader sense of computation made available near data sources, 
 while \gls{ECC} denotes the entire heterogeneous execution environment. 
% 
The distinction that matters is not the label assigned to a particular machine, 
 but the set of trade-offs associated with executing a given computation there.

\subsection{Application Placement and Computation Offloading}
\label{subsec:ecc-offloading}

Using the continuum requires separating the logical organization 
 of an application from its physical deployment. 
% 
Application components have functional responsibilities and data dependencies, 
 whereas infrastructure nodes provide the resources on which component instances execute. 
% 
A \emph{placement} associates the two. 
%
The device to which a computation logically belongs need not be the device that executes it: 
 when execution is transferred to another resource, the computation is said to be \emph{offloaded}.

An offloading decision must determine what to move, where to execute it,
 and when the placement should change. 
%
Existing approaches can be characterized along several dimensions: 
 tasks may be independent or connected into workflows; 
 offloading may move an entire application or only some of its components;
 decisions may be static or revised at runtime; 
 control may be centralized or distributed; 
 and communication may occur through direct or multi-hop connections~\cite{DBLP:journals/access/WangWHSQ20}. 
% 
These dimensions are not independent. 
%
A distributed strategy for partial offloading over a multi-hop network, for example, 
 must account for both component dependencies and the limited information available to each decision maker.

\emph{Full offloading} treats an application or task as one indivisible unit. 
%
It simplifies placement, 
 but offers only a coarse choice between local and remote execution. 
%
\emph{Partial offloading} instead decomposes an application 
 and places its components separately. 
% 
This permits resource-intensive functions to use edge or cloud resources 
 while latency-sensitive functions remain close to their inputs and actuators. 
% 
It also increases the decision space and makes the application structure relevant: 
 placing dependent components far apart can introduce more communication overhead 
 than remote execution saves in computation. 
% 
Component boundaries, data volumes, execution order, and state-management requirements 
 must therefore be considered together with resource availability~\cite{DBLP:journals/access/WangWHSQ20}.

Application decomposition can also make deployment an explicit runtime concern.
%
For example, 
 pulverization separates application logic into independently deployable components 
 so that their allocation can be changed without redefining the behavior of the application itself~\cite{DBLP:journals/fgcs/FarabegoliPCV24}.
%
This separation is particularly relevant to adaptive systems: 
 the logical computation expresses \emph{what} the system should perform, 
 while the deployment mechanism determines \emph{where} each part should run under current conditions.

Offloading is beneficial only when its end-to-end consequences justify the transfer. 
%
Remote execution may reduce local processor use while adding serialization, transmission, queueing, 
 and result-delivery delays. 
% 
It may preserve the battery of an end device but increase radio use, infrastructure cost, 
 or exposure of data. 
% 
Similarly, 
 a powerful server may complete one task quickly 
 but provide poor response times once contention from other devices is considered.
%
Accordingly, 
 placement cannot be decided solely by comparing processor speeds;
 it is a multi-objective decision involving computation, communication, 
 and the state of shared resources~\cite{DBLP:journals/cn/SaeikASSDVLAMP21}.
