\section{Context and Motivation}

% Contesto: Collective Adaptive Systems 
% Negli ultimi anni abbiamo visto un aumento esponenziale di dispositivi connessi a internet, che generano una grande quantità di dati e che sono in grado di prendere decisioni autonome. 
% I sistemi composti da questi dispositivi hanno caratteristiche che li rendono difficili da ingegnerizzare, e che li rendono interessanti da studiare
% Queste caratteristiche sono:
% - Complessità
% - Sono distribuiti (questo porta anche a raccogliere dati non-iid)
% - Open: dispositivi che entrano ed escono dalla rete
% - Località: i dispositivi hanno una visione locale del sistema e non globale, possono parlare solo con alcuni vicini
% - Scala: spesso abbiamo a che fare con centinaia o migliaia di dispositivi (many agents), mentre multi-agents sono più nell'ordine della decina
% - Problemi di privacy: essendo distribuiti sul territorio e pervasivi nelle nostre vite spesso collezionano dati sensibili
% I collective adaptive systems hanno preso piede come paradigma per descrivere questo tipo di sistemi 
% In questi sistemi il tutto è più della somma delle parti, parte del comportamento emerge dall'interazione tra i dispositivi
% Si può vedere i moderni sistemi iot/edge-cloud/smart city come esempio di CAS
% Queste caratteristiche rendono difficile ingegnerizzare un processo di apprendimento in questo contesto, non possiamo prendere algoritmi di AI/ML e applicarli as-is
The last decade has seen a rapid growth in the number 
 of networked devices embedded in physical environments. 
%
These devices continuously generate data, 
 communicate with one another, 
 and increasingly make autonomous decisions. 
% 
Rather than operating in isolation, 
 they form large distributed systems that support applications such as smart cities, 
 pervasive sensing, robotic swarms, and the edge--cloud continuum.

Engineering these systems is difficult because several properties must be addressed at the same time. 
%
First, 
 their behaviour results from the interaction of many components and is therefore difficult to predict 
 from the behaviour of an individual component alone. 
%
Second, computation, communication, and data are distributed: 
 each device has access only to its local state and to information received from a limited set of neighbours. 
%
Consequently, 
 data collected across the system often do not follow a common independent and identically distributed (IID) process, 
 since devices located in different environments observe different phenomena. 
% 
Third, 
 these systems are open and dynamic, as devices may join, leave, fail, or move while the system is running. 
% 
Fourth, 
 they operate at a scale that may involve hundreds or thousands of devices. 
% 
This motivates the term \emph{many-agent}, 
 in contrast to settings involving only a small number of interacting agents. 
% 
Finally, 
 many of these devices are resource-constrained 
 and collect sensitive data because they are distributed throughout physical environments 
 and are closely integrated into everyday life.

Collective Adaptive Systems (CASs) provide a suitable model for this class of systems. 
%
A CAS consists of multiple autonomous or semi-autonomous entities that interact locally 
 and adapt their behaviour while pursuing individual or collective objectives. 
% 
Its global behaviour is not imposed entirely by a central controller; 
%
instead, 
 it partly emerges from the repeated interactions among its components. 
% 
Modern Internet of Things deployments, smart-city infrastructures, robotic collectives, 
 and edge--cloud systems can all be viewed through this lens.

Existing approaches to engineering distributed and adaptive systems make different choices 
 about how the system and its components are represented.
% 
Some adopt the individual device as their main abstraction, 
 whereas others describe the collective as a whole. 
% 
Components may be modelled as passive computational objects or as autonomous agents. 
%
Their coordination may be centralized, decentralized, or hybrid,
 and autonomous behaviour may be expressed through rules, models, policies, or explicit objectives. 
% 
These choices determine what information is available to each component, 
 where decisions are made, and how the resulting global behaviour can be controlled.

Within this broad design space, 
 machine learning has become an increasingly common mechanism for achieving adaptation. 
% 
Yet learning in a CAS introduces requirements that are not adequately captured when 
 an algorithm is considered independently of the system in which it will operate. 
% 
This thesis identifies four engineering dimensions, summarized in Figure~\Cref{fig:overview}.

\emph{Data privacy constraints} concerns how agents can benefit from data distributed across the collective without requiring raw, 
 potentially sensitive observations to be centralized. 
% 
\emph{Spatio-temporal distribution shift} concerns the statistical heterogeneity induced by the environment: 
 nearby devices may observe similar data, distant devices may observe different distributions, 
 and mobile devices may encounter a sequence of distributions over time. 
% 
\emph{Scalable decentralization} concerns how learning and coordination can rely on local interactions 
 while remaining effective as the number of agents grows and the communication topology changes. 
% 
Finally, 
 \emph{resource-constrained edge devices} concerns the computational, memory, energy,
 and communication limitations that restrict both learning and execution at the network edge.

These dimensions are not independent.
%
Privacy constraints restrict which information can be exchanged;
decentralization and locality determine how knowledge propagates;
distribution shifts require models and collaboration structures to adapt;
and resource constraints limit the complexity and frequency of these operations.
%
Taken together, they expose a fundamental mismatch between the assumptions of conventional machine-learning algorithms
and the operating conditions of CASs.
%
Data and global state may be unavailable at a central location,
the set of participating agents may change over time,
and both learning and execution must often take place on resource-constrained devices.
%
Consequently, machine-learning algorithms cannot simply be applied as-is.

Existing work often addresses these challenges in isolation
or introduces solutions tailored to specific applications.
%
What remains missing is a systematic approach to engineering cooperative learning around the defining properties of CASs,
together with reusable abstractions and design strategies that reduce reliance on ad-hoc solutions.
%
The learning process must therefore be treated as an integral part of the collective system,
rather than as an algorithmic component added independently of the system in which it operates.

Starting from the research gap outlined above,
 this thesis investigates how cooperative learning can be engineered as an integral part of a CAS.
%
The objective is not to define a single learning algorithm applicable to every collective system,
 but to identify coordination mechanisms, learning architectures,
 and deployment strategies that address the main challenges arising from distribution,
 locality, scale, openness, and resource constraints.

The investigation is guided by the following research questions:

\begin{questions}
\item
How can privacy-aware cooperative learning be organized among many distributed agents
without centralizing raw data or relying on a fixed central coordinator?\label{itm:rq1}

\item
How can cooperative learning account for spatially heterogeneous data
and adapt to temporal distribution shifts caused by mobility and changing environments?\label{itm:rq2}

\item
How can decentralized learning remain effective and scalable
when agents have access only to local observations,
communicate with a limited neighbourhood,
and operate in collectives whose size may change?\label{itm:rq3}

\item
How can learning-based decisions be deployed across heterogeneous and resource-constrained
edge--cloud infrastructures while accounting for both individual device constraints
and collective system-level phenomena?\label{itm:rq4}
```

\end{questions}

These questions are addressed through three complementary lines of research,
 whose relationship with the engineering dimensions identified in the previous section
 is summarized in \Cref{fig:mapping}.

The first line concerns \emph{self-organizing federated learning}
 and addresses~\ref{itm:rq1} and \ref{itm:rq2}.
%
We developed decentralized approaches in which devices autonomously form learning federations
 according to their spatial proximity and the similarity of their data distributions.
%
This enables the training of models specialized for different regions
 without centralizing raw data or relying on a fixed server.
%
We also introduced a benchmark for evaluating learning under spatially structured data heterogeneity
 and extended the proposed approaches to reduce their resource requirements
 and adapt to mobility-induced distribution shifts while retaining previously acquired knowledge.

The second line concerns \emph{scalable decentralized reinforcement learning}
 and addresses \ref{itm:rq3}.
%
We investigated training strategies based on local interactions among neighbouring agents
 and learning architectures designed to operate with variable-sized collectives.
%
These studies evaluate the trade-offs between centralized and fully independent learning
 and assess whether policies trained with a given number of agents
 can remain effective when deployed in larger collectives.

The third line concerns \emph{adaptive deployment across the edge--cloud continuum}
 and addresses \ref{itm:rq4}.
%
We developed a learning-based approach that allows devices
 to make decentralized decisions about where application components should be executed.
%
These decisions account for both local constraints,
 such as energy consumption and computational capacity,
 and collective conditions,
 such as network congestion, latency, and infrastructure load.

Together, these contributions provide methods and design principles
 for integrating learning and collective coordination
 in many-agent systems characterized by distributed data,
 local interactions, dynamic populations, and limited resources.


\section{Thesis Structure}
The remainder of this thesis is organized into two main parts.

\emph{Part~I---Background} introduces the concepts required to frame the contributions.
%
\Cref{chap:context} presents Collective Adaptive Systems,
 self-adaptive systems,
 and the edge--cloud continuum,
 describing the computational setting considered throughout the thesis.
%
\Cref{chap:macroprogramming} introduces macroprogramming
 and the main abstractions used to express and coordinate collective behaviour.
%
\Cref{chap:ml}  provides the machine-learning foundations required by the subsequent chapters.
%
\Cref{chap:federated-learning}  focuses on federated learning,
 with particular attention to decentralized architectures,
 data heterogeneity,
 and clustered approaches.
%
\Cref{chap:reinforcement-learning} introduces reinforcement learning
 and discusses its extension to multi-agent settings,
 including decentralized training and scalability.

\emph{Part~II---Contribution} presents the research developed during this thesis.
%
\Cref{chap:selforg-fl}  introduces the proposed approaches to self-organizing federated learning,
 addressing privacy-aware collaboration
 and spatially and temporally heterogeneous data.
%
\Cref{chap:scalable-rl}  investigates scalable decentralized reinforcement learning,
 focusing on training through local interactions
 and on the deployment of learned policies in collectives of different sizes.
%
\Cref{chap:tasksplacement} studies adaptive deployment across the edge--cloud continuum,
 presenting a learning-based approach for decentralized application offloading
 under device-level and collective resource constraints.
%
Finally,
 \Cref{chap:wrapup} summarizes the main findings,
 discusses the answers to the research questions,
 and outlines directions for future work.
