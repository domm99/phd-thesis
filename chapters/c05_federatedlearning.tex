\chapter{Federated Learning}
\label{chap:federated-learning}
\minitoc
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Training machine learning models on data that is already distributed
 across many devices presents a fundamental tension.
%
Centralizing the data enables standard optimization procedures
 but sacrifices privacy, incurs high communication cost,
 and becomes impractical when datasets are large or legally restricted.
%
\glsentryfull{FL} resolves this tension by keeping data local:
 devices share model updates rather than raw samples,
 and a shared model is built through aggregation rather than
 through data collection~\cite{DBLP:conf/aistats/McMahanMRHA17}.

This chapter introduces the standard FL paradigm
 and then examines why its assumptions are systematically violated
 in the \gls{CAS} setting.
%
It covers the problem of spatially structured data heterogeneity,
 the transition from centralized to decentralized architectures,
 and the family of clustered FL methods that address statistical
 incompatibility among participants.
%
The chapter closes with a preview of the self-organizing approach
 developed in~\Cref{chap:selforg-fl}.

\section{Standard Federated Learning}
\label{sec:fl-standard}

\glsentryfull{FL} was introduced to enable collaborative model training
 without requiring participants to share their raw data~\cite{DBLP:conf/aistats/McMahanMRHA17}.
%
The canonical setting involves a set of \emph{clients}
 $S = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n\}$,
 each holding a local dataset $D_i$,
 and a central \emph{server} responsible for coordinating training.

The learning objective is to minimize the global empirical risk:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{w} \; F(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{|D_i|}{|D|} F_i(w),
\end{equation}
where $F_i(w) = \frac{1}{|D_i|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_i} \ell(w; x, y)$
 is the local loss on client $i$,
 $\ell$ is the per-sample loss function,
 and $|D| = \sum_i |D_i|$ is the total number of training samples.

The most widely used algorithm for this objective is
 \glsentryfull{FedAvg}~\cite{DBLP:conf/aistats/McMahanMRHA17}.
%
Each training round proceeds in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Broadcast}: the server distributes the current global model
    $w^t$ to a selected subset of clients $S^t \subseteq S$.
  \item \emph{Local training}: each selected client $i \in S^t$ initializes
    its local model from $w^t$ and performs $E$ epochs of stochastic
    gradient descent on $D_i$, producing an updated local model $w_i^{t+1}$.
  \item \emph{Upload}: each client sends $w_i^{t+1}$ back to the server.
  \item \emph{Aggregation}: the server computes the new global model as the
    weighted average of the received updates:
    \begin{equation}
      w^{t+1} = \sum_{i \in S^t} \frac{|D_i|}{\sum_{j \in S^t} |D_j|} \; w_i^{t+1}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The round repeats until convergence or a communication budget is exhausted.
%
Because raw data never leaves the client,
 \gls{FL} reduces exposure compared to centralized collection,
 especially when combined with privacy-enhancing mechanisms
 such as differential privacy or secure aggregation~\cite{DBLP:journals/ftml/KairouzMABBBBCC21}.

\section{Limitations in the CAS Setting}
\label{sec:fl-limits}

The centralized client-server model of \gls{FL} carries structural assumptions
 that are often incompatible with the constraints of \glspl{CAS}.

\paragraph{Single point of failure.}
The server aggregates all updates and distributes the global model in every round.
%
In a large-scale deployment spanning thousands of devices,
 the server introduces a communication bottleneck,
 and its unavailability halts the entire learning process.
%
CAS are open systems in which devices may be mobile, intermittently connected,
 or organized across different administrative domains,
 making a permanently available central coordinator unrealistic.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to non-IID data.}
\gls{FedAvg} and its variants converge under the assumption
 that local data distributions are sufficiently similar across clients.
%
When distributions diverge substantially--a condition referred to as
 \emph{non-\gls{IID}}--the weighted average of locally trained models
 may perform poorly on every individual client,
 a phenomenon known as \emph{client drift}~\cite{DBLP:conf/icml/KarimireddyKMRS20}.
%
Algorithms such as FedProx~\cite{DBLP:conf/mlsys/LiSZSTS20} and
 Scaffold~\cite{DBLP:conf/icml/KarimireddyKMRS20} mitigate drift
 by regularizing local updates or correcting gradient variance,
 but they still produce a single shared model
 that may represent no client's data distribution well.

\paragraph{Scale and dynamism.}
A collective may comprise hundreds or thousands of devices
 whose participation changes dynamically.
%
Synchronization between rounds requires waiting for all selected clients,
 introducing latency that scales with collective size.
%
As devices join, leave, or move,
 the set of available participants and their data distributions
 change continuously, which the standard static-aggregation model does not account for.

These limitations are not independent.
%
Addressing data heterogeneity through more local updates
 increases client drift and worsens aggregation quality;
 and decentralizing aggregation to improve resilience
 requires alternative mechanisms for maintaining model coherence.
%
Resolving these tensions jointly, rather than in isolation, is the
 engineering challenge that motivates the approaches described in the rest of this chapter.

\section{Spatial Data Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:fl-heterogeneity}

A key source of non-\gls{IID} data in \glspl{CAS} is the physical environment.
%
Devices are embedded in space and observe phenomena that vary across locations:
 a traffic sensor in a residential district captures different patterns
 from one on a highway, and a temperature sensor on a rooftop differs
 from one at street level.
%
This produces a form of heterogeneity that is not random
 but \emph{spatially structured}~\cite{DBLP:journals/iot/DominiFAVE26,DBLP:journals/jors/DominiIALV26}.

To formalize this structure, consider a spatial area $A = \{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k\}$
 divided into $k$ distinct contiguous regions,
 each associated with a data distribution $P_j$ over some sample space $\mathcal{X}$.
%
A set of devices $S = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n\}$ ($n \gg k$) is deployed across $A$,
 with each device $s_i$ residing in some region $a_j$
 and holding a local dataset $D_i$ drawn from $P_j$.
%
Devices in the same region share a common distribution;
 devices in different regions do not.
%
The heterogeneity condition is captured by requiring distributions to be
 $\varepsilon$-separated under some divergence measure $D(\cdot \| \cdot)$:
\begin{equation}
  \forall\, i \neq j : \; D(P_i \| P_j) \geq \varepsilon.
\end{equation}
An equivalent sample-level characterization~\cite{DBLP:journals/jors/DominiIALV26},
 using a distance metric $m(\cdot, \cdot)$ and a threshold $\delta > 0$, requires:
\begin{equation}
  \forall\, i \neq j,\; \forall\, d \in P_i,\; \forall\, d'' \in P_j :
  \quad m(d, d') \leq \delta < m(d, d''),
\end{equation}
where $d'$ is any sample from the same distribution $P_i$.
%
Both conditions capture the same intuition:
 within a region data are \gls{IID},
 while data from different regions are distinguishable by a margin.
%
This structure has direct consequences for which collaborations are beneficial
 and which are harmful.

When heterogeneity follows this spatial structure,
 not all collaboration is equally beneficial.
%
Averaging model updates from devices in different regions
 may pull the global model toward an average of incompatible distributions,
 producing a solution that fits no region well.
%
Conversely, devices in the same region can benefit substantially from pooling their data,
 since their observations are locally \gls{IID}.
%
The central challenge is therefore not whether to collaborate,
 but \emph{with whom}:
 the ideal collaboration boundary aligns with the latent spatial partition,
 which is not directly observable by any participant.

Evaluating \gls{FL} algorithms under this kind of structure requires benchmarks
 that model spatial partitions explicitly.
%
Standard benchmarks distribute labels with Dirichlet splits that treat devices independently,
 which conflates the effect of label imbalance with genuine spatial heterogeneity.
%
\textsc{ProFed}~\cite{DBLP:journals/jors/DominiIALV26} addresses this by distributing data
 heterogeneously across regions and homogeneously within them,
 supporting multiple skewness strategies (IID, Dirichlet with parameter $\alpha$,
 and hard partitioning) and a configurable number of regions,
 enabling systematic comparison across varying degrees of spatial heterogeneity,
 and providing the evaluation infrastructure used to assess
 the contributions of this thesis.

\section{Decentralized Federated Learning}
\label{sec:fl-decentralized}

The limitations of a fixed central server have motivated
 a family of \emph{decentralized} or \emph{peer-to-peer} \gls{FL} architectures
 in which aggregation is distributed across the network
 rather than concentrated at a single node~\cite{DBLP:conf/dais/HegedusDJ19}.

In the fully decentralized setting (\Cref{fig:fl-centralized}),
 there is no dedicated server.
%
Each device communicates directly with its neighbors,
 exchanges model updates, and performs aggregation locally.
%
The global objective is approached through repeated local aggregation steps
 that gradually mix information across the network.
%
How quickly this mixing proceeds depends on the spectral gap of the communication graph:
 well-connected networks diffuse information faster,
 while sparse or partitioned topologies slow convergence considerably~\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/LianZZHZL17}.

Gossip-based protocols are a common implementation strategy.
%
At each round, each device exchanges its model
 with a randomly or deterministically selected neighbor
 and updates its local model through pairwise averaging.
%
Repeated gossip rounds propagate information across the network
 without requiring global synchronization or a central coordinator.
%
These properties make gossip protocols well-suited to \glspl{CAS},
 where node failures, intermittent connectivity, and topology changes
 are the norm rather than the exception.

Hierarchical and semi-decentralized architectures occupy a middle ground.
%
A subset of nodes acts as local aggregators for their neighborhood,
 while remaining aggregators may or may not communicate with one another.
%
This approach can reduce communication overhead by exploiting locality
 while retaining some of the convergence benefits of hierarchical aggregation.
%
The field-based coordination mechanisms introduced in~\Cref{chap:macroprogramming}
 are particularly suited to implementing such architectures dynamically,
 because leader election, information collection, and model broadcasting
 can all be expressed as compositions of gradient-cast and converge-cast operators.

Removing the central server has a deeper conceptual implication:
 the notion of a single global model becomes ill-defined
 when devices communicate only with their neighbors.
%
In a fully decentralized architecture,
 each device holds a locally aggregated model
 that reflects contributions from a spatially limited neighborhood.
%
Whether this is a limitation or a feature depends on the data distribution:
 if local data are heterogeneous, a locally adapted model may be preferable
 to a single global one.

\section{Clustered Federated Learning}
\label{sec:fl-clustered}

When a single global model cannot fit the diversity of local data distributions,
 a natural alternative is to learn \emph{multiple} models,
 each tailored to a subset of clients with statistically compatible data.
%
\emph{Clustered \gls{FL}}~\cite{DBLP:journals/tit/GhoshCYR22,DBLP:journals/tnn/SattlerMS21}
 achieves this by partitioning clients into groups
 and running a separate federation within each group,
 so that each cluster converges on a model that fits its own data distribution
 rather than compromising across incompatible ones.

The central question is how to determine which clients belong to the same cluster
 without direct access to their data.
%
The Iterative Federated Clustering Algorithm
 (IFCA~\cite{DBLP:journals/tit/GhoshCYR22}) approaches this
 by alternating between assignment and refinement:
 each client evaluates the current cluster models on its local data
 and joins the cluster with the lowest loss,
 then trains locally and uploads its update to the assigned cluster aggregator.
%
Under sufficiently separated distributions,
 this procedure converges to cluster-specific models
 that outperform a single global model.
%
A related approach~\cite{DBLP:journals/tnn/SattlerMS21}
 identifies clusters in a post-hoc manner
 by computing pairwise cosine similarities between client gradient updates:
 clients with compatible data distributions tend to produce similar gradients,
 so hierarchical clustering on the server can recover the group structure
 without requiring clients to evaluate multiple candidate models.

Underlying these approaches is a more general insight.
%
Clustered \gls{FL} can be understood as a form of model personalization,
 where the degree of personalization is controlled by cluster granularity.
%
A single shared model and fully local training sit at the two extremes;
 clustered approaches occupy the middle ground,
 sharing model capacity among clients with compatible data
 while maintaining distinct models for incompatible groups.
%
This picture, however, has a persistent limitation:
 the number of clusters must be supplied as a hyperparameter.
%
In open and dynamic environments, the natural group structure may be unknown a priori,
 variable over time, or spatially organized in ways not visible to a central server.

\section{Toward Self-Organizing Federated Learning}
\label{sec:fl-selforg-preview}

The approaches reviewed above address individual aspects
 of federated learning in heterogeneous and dynamic settings,
 but they are typically studied under simplified assumptions:
 a fixed cluster count, a known topology, or a static population.
%
In a \gls{CAS}, these assumptions fail simultaneously.

The contribution developed in~\Cref{chap:selforg-fl} addresses this
 by integrating \gls{FL} with the field-based coordination mechanisms
 introduced in~\Cref{chap:macroprogramming}.
%
Rather than treating the clustering structure as a hyperparameter or a preprocessing step,
 the approach builds it dynamically through self-organizing field computations:
 devices discover their collaborators through local interactions,
 elect region leaders without a central coordinator,
 and adapt their federation membership as spatial context and model similarity change.
%
Model sparsification is incorporated directly into the self-organization process,
 allowing the network structure to reflect both distributional similarity
 and resource availability.

Together, these mechanisms yield a family of methods for
 \emph{self-organizing federated learning} that operates under
 the distribution, locality, mobility, and resource constraints
 that characterize real-world \glspl{CAS}.
