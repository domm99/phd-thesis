\begin{abstract}
\Glspl{CAS} comprise distributed devices or agents whose local interactions
give rise to emergent collective behavior that cannot be attributed to any component in isolation.
%
Such behavior can be engineered through explicit coordination rules,
expressed for instance through macroprogramming,
or through machine learning mechanisms that derive models and decision policies from distributed data and experience.
%
This thesis brings these two perspectives together
to investigate how cooperative learning
and decision making can be engineered as integral parts of a \gls{CAS}.
%
This requires addressing conditions that conventional machine learning methods often abstract away,
including heterogeneous and distributed data, local communication, changing populations,
and limited computational resources.
%
Addressing these conditions also requires practical tools
for implementing coordination mechanisms, integrating them with learning algorithms,
and evaluating their collective behavior.
%
The thesis therefore contributes complementary software tools
for aggregate programming, spatially structured data benchmarks,
simulation-based learning, and execution on physical robots.

Three complementary lines of research investigate the associated learning and coordination challenges.
%
To address data locality and privacy requirements,
federated learning enables devices to learn collaboratively while keeping raw data local.
%
However, heterogeneous data and changing populations require the collaboration structure itself to adapt.
%
The first line of research therefore develops field-based coordination mechanisms
that enable devices to form and reorganize federated learning groups
according to spatial proximity and model compatibility,
without relying on a permanent server or fixing the number of groups in advance.
%
Simulation results show that this organization supports specialized learning
and maintains effective collaboration under failures and mobility.
%
Model sparsification can preserve these benefits while reducing computational requirements,
although excessive compression can undermine the stability of self-organization.
%
Preliminary extensions further investigate task-independent collaborator discovery
and continual adaptation to mobility-induced distribution shifts.

To learn cooperative actions from experience under partial observations,
the second line of research investigates decentralized reinforcement learning.
%
Neighbor-based training strategies allow agents to exchange knowledge locally,
while graph-based policies accommodate variable-sized interaction neighborhoods.
%
Experiments show that local knowledge exchange improves decentralized training
and that graph-based policies support transfer across different collective sizes,
although performance degrades as deployment conditions diverge from the training configuration.

To address limited device resources and contention for shared infrastructure,
the third line of research investigates adaptive application deployment across the edge--cloud continuum.
%
A graph-based reinforcement learning approach combines local device information
with aggregate estimates of collective conditions
to decide where application components should execute.
%
Simulation experiments show that collective information enables decentralized policies
to adapt placement decisions to shared operating conditions,
balancing device energy consumption, infrastructure costs, and server load.
%
The policies also adjust their decisions as devices move between areas with different collective conditions.

Together, these methods, tools, and empirical findings show
how explicitly representing and organizing the structure of the collective
can support cooperative learning and decentralized decision making
under the operating conditions of \glspl{CAS}.

\end{abstract}
