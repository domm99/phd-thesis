\begin{abstract}
\glspl{CAS} comprise devices whose local observations 
 and actions jointly shape the behavior of the collective. 
Enabling them to learn and act together is difficult: 
 data are distributed and heterogeneous, communication is local, 
 populations change, and computational resources are limited. 
Conventional learning methods, by contrast, 
 often assume centralized coordination, a fixed population, or a shared data distribution. 
\emph{This thesis investigates how cooperative learning and decision making 
 can be engineered under the conditions of a \gls{CAS}, 
 treating the structure of the collective as a first-class element of the learning system.}

Three complementary lines of research address different aspects of this problem. 
First, 
 field-based coordination lets devices form and reorganize federated learning groups 
 according to spatial proximity and model compatibility, 
 without pooling raw data, relying on a permanent central server, 
 or fixing the number of groups in advance. 
This mechanism is extended with model sparsification 
 and supported by a benchmark for spatially structured data heterogeneity. 
Second, 
 decentralized reinforcement learning is studied along two dimensions: 
 the exchange of experiences or parameters among neighbors during training, 
 and graph-based policies that can be executed by swarms of different sizes without retraining. 
Third, 
 a graph-based offloading approach combines local device information 
 with aggregate estimates of shared conditions 
 to place application components across edge and cloud resources.

The methods are evaluated in simulation. 
Self-organizing federations learn models specialized to spatially heterogeneous data, 
 outperform single-model baselines under strong label skew, 
 match a clustered baseline that is given the correct number of clusters, 
 and reorganize after leader failures and device movement. 
Moderate sparsification preserves this behavior, 
 whereas aggressive pruning fragments the federations. 
Preliminary results further indicate that continual learning can mitigate forgetting 
 when devices move between regions, 
 and that lightweight novelty estimators can identify compatible collaborators 
 independently of the task model. 
In the evaluated coordination task, 
 neighbor exchanges bring decentralized learners close to centrally trained ones, 
 with experience sharing incurring the lowest communication cost; 
 graph-based policies trained with ten agents remain functional in swarms 
 up to about three times larger, 
 although their efficiency declines as the population grows. 
Finally, 
 collective density information enables offloading decisions 
 that differ between congested and sparse areas, 
 a differentiation that agents do not learn when this information is removed. 
Together, 
 these findings show that making collective structure explicit 
 improves learning and decentralized decisions in each setting.
\end{abstract}
